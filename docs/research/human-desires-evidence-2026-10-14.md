# Human desires that can make Allworld worth returning to

Research memo, 14 October 2026. This is a design and measurement brief, not a claim that every player shares one motive or that any feature causes retention. The evidence is strongest for three psychological needs in Self-Determination Theory (SDT): autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Status, identity/expression, curiosity, agency/ownership, purpose/contribution, and safety/predictability/comfort are useful design motives or affordances, but this source set does not establish them as universal basic needs in a fixed hierarchy.

## What this means for Allworld mechanics

- **Autonomy and agency/ownership:** Let a player choose routes, pace, neighborhoods, missions, companions, and how a home or character is expressed. Agency is satisfied when choices are consequential and self-endorsed; it is frustrated by forced funnels, arbitrary resets, and “daily” obligations that feel compulsory.
- **Competence and achievement:** Streaks, achievements, missions, maps, and skill feedback can make progress visible. The healthy version gives informative feedback and achievable next steps. The frustrating version uses opaque rules, grind, public failure, or rewards that make the player feel controlled. A badge is evidence of progress only if the player understands what skill or contribution it represents.
- **Relatedness and social identity:** Friends, resident relationships, cooperative missions, recognition, and shared local stories can create belonging. AI residents can support rehearsal, companionship, and continuity, but they should not impersonate human intimacy or imply a relationship the system cannot sustain. Social features must allow opting out and avoid punishing players for unavailable friends.
- **Curiosity and immersion:** A recognizable but incomplete place, a resident with a question, or a route that opens onto a meaningful local detail can create an information gap. Close the loop with discovery, explanation, or consequence. Endless locked content and spoiler-resistant opacity may produce clicks while undermining trust.
- **Purpose and contribution:** Let players improve a neighborhood, document a place, help a resident, or leave something useful for another player. This is a design hypothesis about meaning and contribution, not a demonstrated universal need in this evidence set. Track whether contribution feels voluntary and valued.
- **Status and identity/expression:** Titles, collections, avatar choices, homes, and public recognition can signal competence or identity to self and others. Status systems can also trigger comparison and exclusion; make recognition plural, optional, and grounded in real accomplishments rather than pure spend or grind.
- **Safety, predictability, and comfort:** Clear rules, recoverable mistakes, stable navigation, consent controls, moderation, and reliable resident behavior reduce uncertainty. Treat these as enabling conditions and player preferences, not as proof of a Maslow-style bottom layer. Predictability should coexist with enough novelty for curiosity.

## Three testable product hypotheses

1. **Choice plus competence:** For new players, a mission flow offering two or three genuinely different routes with clear skill feedback will increase post-session perceived autonomy and competence, and improve voluntary return within seven days, compared with a single prescribed route. Measure validated short-form autonomy/competence items immediately after play, route choice, completion quality, and seven-day return; preregister that return is an outcome, not proof of need satisfaction.
2. **Human social continuity:** Players who complete an optional cooperative neighborhood task with a real friend or stable player group will report higher relatedness and show more repeat co-play over 14 days than players who complete an equivalent solo task. Measure relationship quality, invitations accepted, co-play days, and attrition; do not interpret correlation as proof that the social feature caused retention.
3. **Curiosity with payoff:** A resident-led mystery that gives a concrete clue, allows player-directed exploration, and resolves within one or two sessions will increase curiosity and next-session intent without increasing frustration, compared with an equally long mission with no information gap. Measure curiosity, perceived control, frustration, resolution recall, next-session intent, and return; include an opt-out and compare across player cultures/cohorts.

## Guardrails for interpretation

- These sources describe motives, associations, and bounded experimental effects. They do not establish that streaks, badges, missions, relationships, or AI residents cause retention, addiction, or well-being.
- Do not claim that one hierarchy explains all humans. The Maslow hierarchy has only partial empirical support, and even SDT’s universality claims remain debated and culturally qualified.
- Use player-reported need satisfaction/frustration, behavior, and qualitative evidence together. Session length and return are behavioral outcomes with many causes, including convenience, habit, social obligation, novelty, and product quality.
- Report heterogeneity: age, culture, prior game experience, disability/access needs, social availability, and player intent can change how the same mechanic is experienced.
